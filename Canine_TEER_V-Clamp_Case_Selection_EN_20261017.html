Case Selection Criteria for Canine Mitral TEER (V-Clamp)

犬二尖瓣 TEER（V-Clamp）病例筛选标准总结
Prepared by Xufeng Ying
Science and Innovation for Animal Health Program, Graduate School, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Kasetsart University, Bangkok 10900, Thailand
Evidence cut-off: October 2026 ｜ Base: 13 publications (3 methodological reviews, 1 four-tier complexity grading) ｜ Version 2026-10-17

Three questions this document answers

(1) Who is suitable — what inclusion criteria have actually been applied; (2) who is unsuitable — which anatomical and clinical features are consistently regarded as exclusionary or high-risk; (3) on what evidence — the original source and strength of every statement.

The document is in two halves. Sections 1–7 are a paper-by-paper comparison, placing each study's inclusion criteria, exclusions and thresholds side by side for verification. Section 8 is a consolidated checklist, reducing that evidence to a workflow usable in the consulting room and the pre-procedural discussion, with a reference number after every item.

A note on the evidence base: all anatomical thresholds in canine TEER currently rest on clinical experience in fewer than 300 dogs, with no randomised evidence. Most "criteria" are cautionary lines formed retrospectively from failed cases rather than validated algorithms. They should be used as decision support, not as gatekeeping rules.

What the table shows: only one of 13 publications [1] presents complete predefined eligibility criteria; two [8][10] explicitly acknowledge having none, and [8] reports 35% early mortality. Three common threads emerge: (1) the stage threshold is uniform — B2, C and early D form the shared window, with B1 confined to early exploratory work; (2) the severity threshold is uniform — E-wave >1 m/s, RF >50% and RVol >1.0 mL/kg recur throughout, all derived from canine adaptations of the ASE integrative guidelines; (3) the anatomical threshold is converging — central A2/P2 is the accepted target and an AP annular dimension of 14–22 mm is the device constraint.

Two conclusions follow. (1) The three clinical exclusions — TR-PG >80 mmHg, non-cardiac disease limiting 6-month survival, and active infection or immune-mediated disease — are identical wherever predefined criteria exist and can be treated as a minimum consensus. (2) Every anatomical exclusion in the literature originates from retrospective review of failures; none came from prospective design. That is precisely the source of the three preventable deaths in [8].

Practical note: the original footnote states "optimal clamp size = mid-systolic AP dimension ± 1 mm", which differs by 1 mm from the "±0" reported later in [2][3]. The authors also stress that complexity is a continuum shaped by risk, benefit and centre experience, not a binary gate.

6. Quantifying regurgitation severity

Del Nero 2025 (51 dogs, single operator) provides the most complete canine methodology and reference intervals to date

6.1 The two methods are not interchangeable

Volumetric quantification (single-plane Simpson total stroke volume minus aortic VTI × annular area) and PISA differ systematically: volumetric values run 0.24 mL/kg lower for regurgitant volume and 13.7% lower for regurgitant fraction, with very wide limits of agreement (RVol −1.45 to 0.97 mL/kg; RF −60.7 to 33.4%). Whichever method is chosen at baseline must be used throughout follow-up; otherwise results cannot be compared.

6.2 Four methodological constraints for canine TEER

  • Use the volumetric method as the primary baseline measure. Inter-observer ICC for volumetric RVol was 0.98; for PISA-derived RF it was 0.70, the weakest of the four indices.
  • PISA cannot be used after TEER. The procedure creates a double-orifice valve, invalidating the geometric assumptions of PISA — an explicit recommendation in human post-procedural guidelines that applies equally to dogs.
  • Multiple jets and chordal rupture are hard boundaries for quantification. PISA fails in both; the original study excluded them. Assess whether the number is trustworthy before interpreting it.
  • Adapt the views for dogs. The study used the human-standard left apical four-chamber view for total stroke volume; the authors subsequently recommended the right parasternal long-axis view (less foreshortening) or measuring in both views and taking the larger value.
Avoid this mis-citation: the study provides stage-wise distributions only. It does not define formal mild/moderate/severe cut-offs for dogs, and contains no canine 3D VCA values. "RVol ≥1.5 mL/kg" should be described as corresponding closely to stage C, not as an official threshold.

7. Imaging chain: TTE → TEE → CT

Three steps answering three different questions; none substitutes for another

7.1 TTE — severity, stage, initial annular measurement

Standard transthoracic echocardiography establishes three things: severity by integrative assessment (the five criteria used in [1][2] appear in Section 4), ACVIM stage, and the mid-systolic AP annular dimension from the left apical inflow-outflow view. The AP value at this stage is for screening only and must be confirmed on TEE before device sizing [1].

7.2 TEE — anatomy, device, guidance

[3] reduces candidacy to three elements — anatomy, severity and annular dimension — and provides a reproducible sequence: 3D imaging first (commissural view to locate jet number and position) → 2D biplane (commissural as the primary view, orthogonal IO view for AP dimension, leaflet length and coaptation gap) → segmental sweep A1–P1 / A2–P2 / A3–P3 → baseline 3D colour VCA. Timing is specified: AP dimension in early systole between hinge points; leaflet length in late diastole at full extension.

Safety of this step has independent support: [13] studied intraprocedural TEE in 60 dogs of median weight 3.8 kg and found new mucosal lesions in 20, all minor, with no complex injury and minimal haemodynamic change. TEE is therefore feasible in very small, cardiomegalic and heparinised patients, provided probe size is matched to body weight and temperature and vagal responses are monitored.

7.3 CT — a planning tool, not a selection gate

[7] reviews cardiac CT of the canine mitral and tricuspid valves, and its boundaries must be stated plainly: the article reports no reference values for the normal canine mitral complex — no annular area, circumference or AP diameter, no leaflet lengths, no body-weight-normalised figures. CT therefore cannot currently supply any selection threshold. Three uses do have explicit textual support:

  • Virtual TEE simulation: defining oesophageal course and a virtual probe position to predict the angulation, rotation and flexion needed for optimal planes — particularly valuable in dogs, where the oesophagus is narrow and probe choice limited;
  • Virtual device implantation: testing size suitability and predicting outflow tract obstruction on a 3D dataset;
  • Mechanism identification: regurgitation unexplained by transthoracic echo (for example, aberrant chordae restricting a leaflet) can be demonstrated by CT, indicating segments to avoid when placing a clamp.

The authors' laboratory data further suggest that dogs with degenerative mitral disease show systolic annular expansion (rather than normal systolic reduction), reversible with pimobendan. This aligns with current practice of sizing on the systolic AP dimension, but the original article does not draw that inference; any such statement should be marked as extrapolation.

Conclusion: TEE is the eligibility gate and the procedural guide; TTE is the screening and follow-up tool; CT is an adjunct for pre-procedural planning and difficult mechanisms. They are not interchangeable, and there is no evidence base for substituting CT for TEE in selection.

Step 2 — Is severe regurgitation objectively established?

  • All three quantitative gates met: E-wave >1 m/s, RF >50%, RVol >1.0 mL/kg [1]; or a majority of integrative severe-MR criteria [2].
  • Fix the method at baseline: volumetric preferred, recording view and formula, and use the same method at follow-up [6].
  • Judge whether the number is trustworthy first: with two or more jets or chordal rupture, both PISA and volumetric methods may fail; use 3D VCA and multiparametric integration rather than a single figure [6].
  • Do not exclude on diuretic status or on the owner's perception of symptoms — symptom assessment in dogs is unreliable and objective criteria take precedence [4].

Step 5 — Perioperative risk and team capability

  • Matching the clinical window to the centre matters more than finding the "best" centre. Dogs that can wait may be referred to a high-volume centre; those that cannot should go to a qualified centre with capacity, with the centre's experience and published outcome range disclosed [5].
  • A new centre should confine its first 25 cases to non-complex anatomy [4]; the human learning curve plateaus at roughly 50 cases.
  • Requirements: general anaesthesia with TEE including 3D, appropriate probe sizes, fluoroscopy, 24–48 h post-procedural monitoring, and three months of antiplatelet therapy (clopidogrel 2–3 mg/kg) [1][2].
  • Pre-procedural discussion must state that TEER aims to reduce rather than eliminate regurgitation, that echocardiographic follow-up is lifelong, and that some cases require a second clamp [2][5].

Step 6 — Objective criteria for procedural success

  • Technical success: device implanted, residual MR reduced by at least one grade, no major intraoperative complication [12].
  • Haemodynamic: post-procedural mean MVPG <5 mmHg; capture length ≥4 mm per leaflet [1][3][4].
  • Early efficacy target: RF ≤40% (risk of cardiac death rises markedly above 40%) [2]; intraprocedural 3D VCA reduced by >50% [3].

9. Limitations

  • Small evidence base. All canine TEER cases cited here total fewer than 300, with no randomised data. Current thresholds derive largely from single-centre experience and retrospective review of failures; [5] itself describes its framework as reasoning support rather than a validated algorithm.
  • No prospective validation of the complexity grading. The four tiers represent collective experience from four centres and have not been validated prospectively; the authors state the criteria will continue to evolve.
  • Structural weakness in quantification. There is no gold standard for left ventricular stroke volume in dogs. Neither volumetric nor PISA measurement has validated accuracy, and negative regurgitant fractions occurred in B1 dogs, indicating that noise exceeds signal at the mild end.
  • No canine CT reference values. No CT-based selection threshold is given here because none exists in the literature; any future quantitative CT study of the normal canine annulus and leaflets should be incorporated.
  • Body size. All evidence comes from small-to-medium dogs (median weight 3.8–8.1 kg); extrapolation to large breeds requires caution.

This document is decision-support material and does not constitute medical advice for any individual case. Final judgement rests with a cardiology and interventional team experienced in canine TEER, after complete assessment.
