PART Ⅰ　Human (Chapters 1–7) — How It Is Done in Humans

1. Disease Background and Treatment Landscape of Human Mitral Regurgitation ■

1.1 Disease classification: primary (degenerative) and secondary (functional) MR are two different evidence lines

  • Primary/degenerative MR (DMR): disease of the leaflet itself (prolapse, flail, myxomatous degeneration) → structurally "repairable", surgery is first choice. This corresponds to the homologous pathology of canine MMVD (see Chapter 10) — the single most important human disease prototype for canine translation.
  • Secondary/functional MR (FMR): the leaflets themselves are normal; LV dilation/papillary displacement tethers them → the ventricle is what is being treated. The opposite conclusions of MITRA-FR and COAPT are rooted precisely in population selection (see Chapter 3).

1.2 The treatment landscape and where TEER sits

  • Surgical repair/replacement: the gold standard, but a substantial share of patients are judged "high surgical risk" (advanced age, low LVEF, comorbidities) and denied surgery.
  • Drugs (GDMT): the foundation for FMR, but they do not solve the mechanical problem.
  • TEER: the minimally invasive alternative for high-risk/inoperable patients; within two decades it moved from technical exploration to guideline recommendation (DMR high surgical risk Ⅱa; secondary MR meeting COAPT criteria Ⅱa — ESC/AHA wording □ to verify).

2. Technical Principles and Brief History of TEER ◐

1991–2001 — Alfieri proposes the surgical "edge-to-edge suture" (double-orifice conversion): suturing the midpoints of the anterior and posterior leaflets together. Technically simple but disrupts normal valve physiology; long-term function is maintained by the double orifice — the principle starting point of all TEER.
2003 — First percutaneous edge-to-edge clip implantation in humans (MitraClip precursor); the EVEREST I feasibility study begins.
2008 → — MitraClip CE mark; the transfemoral–transseptal approach is standardized.
2011 / 2013 — EVEREST II RCT published (NEJM): superior safety, effectiveness non-inferiority not met but 5-year outcomes converge (Chapter 3) — TEER formally enters the guideline system.
2018 — COAPT positive result (FMR, NEJM) and MITRA-FR negative result (JACC) published the same year — "patient selection matters more than device performance" becomes field-wide consensus (Chapter 6).
2018–2023 — Device iteration: MitraClip G4 four-arm family; the domestic (Chinese) ValveClamp first case (2018.07, Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University) → FIM (2019, JACC CI) → CLAMP-2 (2023) → NMPA approval (2023.09, the first independently developed Chinese mitral intervention device); tricuspid TEER (TriClip, TRILUMINATE 2023) and PASCAL (CLASP series) advance in parallel.
2020 → — Human devices spill over into veterinary medicine: the human ValveClamp adapted for 8 dogs with spontaneous MR (Front Vet Sci 2020, the world's first canine TEER paper) → the canine-dedicated V-Clamp → global clinical translation at CSU (Chapter 9).

5. Imaging Guidance and Procedural Workflow ◐

5.1 The dual-track approach

  • Transfemoral–transseptal (MitraClip/PASCAL/TriClip): least invasive, but requires fluoroscopy/radiation and transseptal puncture skills; the delivery system's freedom of motion across the septum is constrained.
  • Transapical (ValveClamp/V-Clamp): straight-line alignment with the mitral valve, the most natural force axis, and ultrasound-only, radiation-free guidance — precisely the engineering reason it can migrate to veterinary medicine (small canine hearts; fluoroscopy is a scarce resource in veterinary hospitals).

5.2 Imaging guidance essentials (the TEE step sequence)

  • Preprocedural TEE screening (anatomic enrollment, Chapter 6) → intraprocedural real-time 3D TEE guidance: transseptal puncture position → delivery system crossing the valve → clip-arm orientation (perpendicular to the coaptation line) → the four-step assessment of position/grasp/closure/release (grasped tissue length, double-orifice formation, residual MR, transvalvular gradient).
  • ValveClamp-system companion protocols: the stepwise TEE operating standard (Ge 2021, J Interv Cardiol, PMID 33976590) and the real-time guidance strategy for eccentric jets (A1/A3/P1) (Long 2021, J Card Surg, PMID 34396585) — the latter breaks the "A2-P2 only" restriction and is directly usable for choosing where to grasp diseased canine leaflets.

5.3 Learning curve and team

  • CLAMP-2: 1-year effectiveness rose from 80% to 96.1% after ≈50 cases — the golden quantitative reference for training; the multidisciplinary care model (PMID 34321032) provides a team-building template.

6. Case Selection Criteria: The Field-Wide "First Determinant" ◐

Core fact: MITRA-FR and COAPT reached opposite conclusions → post-hoc analyses pointed to population/anatomic selection differences → case selection was established as the first determinant of TEER outcomes. The same logic is validated in the dog: the CSU 111-dog highly selected cohort (93.7% discharge survival) vs the Korean 25-dog broad-inclusion series (65% early survival; see 9.2 Team 2 and Chapter 13).

7.2 Cross-comparison: what this pipeline has solved, and what it still lacks

  • Solved: a fully open-source pipeline (imaging → modeling → solving → metrics) with zero commercial dependencies; virtual repair can compare TEER/annuloplasty/combined in one frame; three independent routes to strain estimation (FEM shape enforcement / pure-imaging tracking / FEM-enhanced registration).
  • Hard limits: ① geometries mostly modified from healthy valves; ② isotropic Lee–Sacks constitutive model with adult parameters; ③ no pre/post paired validation (ADEPT, arXiv:2409.19081, is filling the "non-invasive elasticity estimation" gap); ④ the annular boundary-condition schools disagree (pinned vs compliance-sensitive).
  • Convergence point of the two lines: image-based direct strain measurement (④⑤) can provide validation data for FEM (①②) — computation and imaging calibrate each other.

PART Ⅱ　Canine (Chapters 8–13) — Feasible Translational Pathways

8. Canine MMVD (Myxomatous Mitral Valve Disease) and the Current Treatment Landscape ◐

8.1 Disease burden

  • MMVD (myxomatous mitral valve disease; older literature: degenerative mitral valve disease, DMVD) is the most common acquired heart disease in dogs; prevalence is high in small/older dogs (ACVIM stages A–F); its natural history is homologous to human degenerative MR — the dog is the only species in which the human-homologous valve disease occurs naturally at scale.

8.2 Current treatment and the gap

  • Drugs: pimobendan as cornerstone (+ diuretics/ACEI, etc.) — improves symptoms and prognosis but does not reverse the disease process.
  • Surgery: open-chest repair on cardiopulmonary bypass (the Japanese Uechi system is the most mature; a few European centers) — very few centers, high barrier, high cost.
  • The interventional gap: TEER fills the middle band of "drugs not enough, surgery too risky" — structurally identical to why human TEER was born.

12. The Feasible Canine Translational Pathway: The Phase 0–4 Roadmap ◐

Core argument (this survey's stance): the dog is not "a lesser human model" but the only natural model that carries the target pathology by itself — every preprocedural simulation tool of human TEER can go further in the dog, because the cases are themselves the source of endpoints; and simulation/printing is simultaneously a direct implementation of the 3R principles (Reduce/Replace/Refine) of animal ethics.

12.1 Case-selection layer

  • Naturally diseased MMVD dogs (ACVIM stage C/D) as subjects; enrollment framework: staging + MR quantification (Del Nero 2025 methodology, PMID 39914360) + leaflet anatomic conditions (prolapse vs tethering, coaptation gap) + annular size window (Willows ≥13 mm as the starting point).
  • Biomarker dimension: NT-proBNP/cTnI (Korea 2025, PMID 40266932) as risk-stratification support. ◐

12.2 Imaging and modeling layer

  • Intraoperative 3D TEE acquisition SOP → SlicerHeart canine adaptation (segmentation/marking/chordal annotation) → an open canine mitral image-geometry library (open-source, cross-center sharing, mirroring the openness of the human FINESSE model library).

12.3 Virtual-repair layer

  • Build the V-Clamp geometry into the library (modeled on Aslan's four G4 geometries) → plan sweeps (model × position × number) → relative rankings of ROA/CA/stress → a virtual-trial report as part of the pre-procedure discussion.
  • Personalized size prediction (Gaidulis paradigm): a retrospective paired study (existing cases' pre-op imaging + actual clip results) first, to verify the model's directional correctness. ◐

12.4 Physical-rehearsal layer

  • 3D printing of diseased canine hearts (material stiffness calibrated ex vivo) → delivery-path and clipping-feel rehearsal → curriculum-based training (against the CLAMP-2 learning curve of ~50 cases).

13.7 Judging "can reproduce": the open-source asset inventory

  • All three elements in place (software + models + pipeline): the CHOP×Utah axis, uniquely — the open-source FEBio solver, the open-source SlicerHeart imaging pipeline, and patient-specific .feb models freely downloadable (repo.febio.org project/137). Any institution — including a veterinary one — can load a canine geometry and start today, with no application to anyone.
  • Open geometry model: UT Austin's Texas TriValve 1.0 (PMID 37139164) — a reverse-engineered open model of the human tricuspid valve, directly usable as the comparator baseline for canine TEER boundary-condition studies.
  • Open solver framework: UNC's whole-heart FSI uses IBAMR (an open-source adaptive FSI framework) — the fluid side is reproducible.
  • Other teams (the Milan lines / Georgia Tech / UCSF / Berlin): methods fully described but no public model repositories — "replicable" (redo from the paper) ≠ "reproducible" (run the artifacts directly). 13.8 From the simulation map to the canine pathway: direct conclusions (closing this survey's core question)

  • Nine-plus active simulation teams worldwide, none with a veterinary application published — "who has simulated the dog" currently has the answer "no one"; but that means the first mover is instantly world-first, and every tool is already free.
  • Adoption-feasibility ranking (by publication evidence): ① CHOP×Utah — the tool triad is complete; only canine geometry and tissue-parameter inputs are missing (closing the Chapter 11 assessment); ② UT Austin Rausch — open TriValve geometry plus boundary-condition sensitivity methodology; best placed to answer "clip mechanics in a small canine annulus"; ③ Emory/Padala — the decision paradigm closest to the operator's use case; ④ the Canon model — manufacturer-owned simulation; a ready industrial path for a V-Clamp manufacturer; ⑤ the Milan lines — the best candidates for independent academic validation with no ties to the device origin.
  • Canine entry-point recommendation: for Phase 0 (Chapter 12), first reuse the CHOP×Utah triad plus the Rausch boundary-condition protocol, starting with "healthy canine geometry + virtual-repair retrospective" — zero licensing cost, zero software cost.
  • Breakthrough point for the Chinese-language region: the region's clinical evidence gap (9.2 Team 9) + its simulation teams just starting (Wuhan Renmin / CAS Institute of Mechanics, 2025) — building the "clinical × simulation" tracks together in the Chinese-language region is the differentiation opportunity, and the path that turns the evidence gap into a publication opportunity.
